Most shareholder disputes we see were drafted into existence years earlier — by silence. The companies that weather founder conflict do so because someone wrote down the rules while everyone was still friendly. Five clauses matter most.

## 1. Deadlock resolution

Equal shareholders eventually disagree, and a company that cannot act bleeds value. A deadlock clause prescribing escalation, then mediation, then a defined buy-sell mechanism converts paralysis into process.

## 2. Transfer restrictions and pre-emption

Shares must not be able to escape to strangers. Pre-emption rights give existing shareholders first refusal at a price set by formula — protecting both control and minority holders from waking up beside an unwelcome co-owner.

## 3. Drag-along and tag-along

Drag rights let majority sellers take minorities with them so a deal can close; tag rights let minorities join a major exit on identical terms. Together they prevent both hostage scenarios — the trapped buyer and the abandoned founder.

## 4. Valuation formula

Every buy-sell and pre-emption clause is worthless without an agreed method of pricing shares: named valuer, discount rules for unlisted stock, and treatment of pending funding rounds.

## 5. Reserved matters

Certain decisions — new debt, director appointments, related-party dealings — require specified majorities regardless of ordinary board authority. This is where minority protection actually lives.
